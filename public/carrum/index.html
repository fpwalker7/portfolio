1Section 1 · Assumptions

Assumptions

Stated up front, as requested:

  • Scale: ~500 resources at launch, growing to 3,000–5,000 over two years. Three content formats: clinical case studies, research papers, webinars.
  • Traffic shape: overwhelmingly anonymous, read-heavy B2B traffic (benefits leaders, health-system stakeholders). Logged-in users are a handful of editors. This asymmetry drives the whole caching strategy.
  • Stack: WordPress on a managed host with server-level page caching and Redis available, a CDN in front, Marketo for marketing automation and Salesforce as the CRM of record. This is the stack I worked with at CyberArk: I owned the web side of the integration, partnering with demand generation and marketing ops, who own the platforms themselves. That division of responsibility is assumed throughout, because it's how enterprise teams actually run.
  • Team: one content manager who is not technical and should never need a developer to publish, with demand generation and marketing ops as partner teams owning campaigns, lead flow, and the automation platforms. My role covers the web layer and the seams to those teams. Plus a design system that must be impossible to break casually.
  • Compliance posture: healthcare-adjacent marketing content, not PHI. But I treat tracking and form data with HIPAA-adjacent caution anyway; it's the right instinct in this space.
2Section 2 · Content architecture

Content architecture: one post type, three taxonomies

The tempting move is three custom post types: case_study, paper, webinar. I'd argue against it. Format is a facet of a resource, not a different kind of thing: everything in this library shares a lifecycle, a card layout, a filtering UI, and a gating strategy.

Decision

A single resource CPT with three registered taxonomies:

  • resource_type: case study / research paper / webinar
  • medical_specialty: oncology, cardiology, musculoskeletal…
  • department: the hospital-department segmentation

Plus a small set of ACF fields per resource (gated asset file, webinar embed, citation metadata) shown conditionally by resource_type.

Why this matters for the brief's three goals:

  • Performance: taxonomy queries hit the indexed wp_term_relationships table, while filtering on meta values means unindexed string comparisons against wp_postmeta: a full table scan at scale. Every filtered view maps to a canonical taxonomy archive URL, which makes it cacheable (Section 3).
  • Editor simplicity: one "Add resource" flow, not three. The content manager picks a type; the right fields appear.
  • Lead capture: gating rules key off resource_type and a per-resource override, in one place (Section 5).
Trade-off I'm accepting
One CPT means less per-format flexibility; a webinar's edit screen carries the shape of the shared model. I mitigate with conditional field groups and format-specific block patterns. If a format later diverges hard (say, webinars become a course platform), I'd split it out then. Don't pre-pay for divergence that hasn't happened.
Field note: CyberArkCyberArk's resource center serves this same content mix (whitepapers, ebooks, webinars, videos) across a large product and solution matrix. The lesson I carry from working on that ecosystem: the taxonomy model is a marketing asset, not just a database decision. Get the segmentation right early and every campaign, nav menu, and filtered landing page inherits it for free; get it wrong and you're bulk-recategorizing thousands of assets later.
Field note: buy vs. buildWorth naming the alternative: dedicated content-hub platforms (Uberflip and similar) exist for exactly this use case, and I've run one at enterprise scale. CyberArk's resource center is hosted on that model. Those platforms buy you a fast launch, easy marketer workflows, and native Marketo integration. The costs: hub content lives on a separate path or subdomain that fragments SEO authority, Core Web Vitals are only as good as the platform allows, design is bound to their templates, and the subscription runs forever. For a hub whose growth engine is thousands of SEO-bearing URLs, I'd build native WordPress: every URL keeps its authority on the main domain, and performance stays fully in our control. That's the trade this whole document is defending.

3Section 3 · Performance

Sub-second at 5,000 posts

WordPress doesn't get slow because of post count. It gets slow because of unindexed meta_query filtering, uncached repeat work, and third-party scripts. So the performance plan is three rules and three layers.

Rule 1: never filter user-facing pages by post meta

Anything a visitor can filter by lives in a taxonomy (indexed, fast at any scale). Post meta is for display data only: file URLs, citations, embed IDs. A meta_query against wp_postmeta on a 5,000-post library is where "sub-second" goes to die.

Rule 2: filters are URLs, not AJAX state

Faceted filtering renders as pre-defined taxonomy archive and intersection URLs. Each is a static-cacheable, SEO-indexable page. An SPA-style filter UI would force every visitor through uncached dynamic queries and hide 5,000 resources from search engines. That is the opposite of a growth asset.

Rule 3: the origin is the last resort

Front-end budget

Performance is also a payload discipline: system font stack (zero font requests), responsive AVIF/WebP images with explicit dimensions, no jQuery dependency in the theme, and a hard budget of ≤ 150 KB critical-path weight, LCP < 1.5 s on 4G, CLS < 0.05, enforced in CI with Lighthouse, not aspirationally in a wiki.

4Section 4 · Editing

Editing: Gutenberg for freedom, tokens for the fence

"Gutenberg vs. ACF Blocks" is a false binary. The real question is where the design system is enforced. My answer: enforce it below the editor, in theme.json and a curated block library, so the content manager gets genuine freedom inside a fence they can't see and can't break.

Decision
  • theme.json owns every token. Brand palette, type scale, spacing scale defined once; custom colors, custom font sizes, and raw CSS input disabled. Off-brand isn't a training issue; it's structurally impossible.
  • Native Gutenberg for narrative content, with an allow-list of ~15 blocks. No one needs seven ways to make a column.
  • ACF Blocks for structured, repeated components: resource cards, gated-download CTA, stat callouts, webinar embeds. Marketers fill fields; the markup is mine, so it's consistent, accessible, and fast everywhere it appears.
  • Block patterns + a locked page template for each resource type, so "publish a new case study" is fill-in-the-blanks, not layout design.
Field note: why I trust this hybridI've shipped on all three generations of WordPress editing. At CyberArk I worked in a WPBakery Page Builder + ACF stack at enterprise scale: fast to assemble, but shortcode lock-in, heavier markup, and brand consistency that depended on editor discipline instead of structure. I've also worked hands-on with native Gutenberg, and seen the opposite failure mode: free-form blocks with no fence. This hybrid is defined by the failure modes I've watched each pure approach produce: Gutenberg for freedom, theme.json for the fence, ACF Blocks where structure matters. And it's the direction WordPress core itself is investing in, so I'm building on the platform's roadmap, not against it.

// Same logic applies to CSS: I've maintained Bootstrap-based WP themes at CyberArk. No framework here: unused CSS on every load, and utility classes live in markup where no theme.json fence governs them. Tokens give me Bootstrap's consistency without its payload. I know the road I'm declining.

Trade-off I'm accepting
ACF Blocks add a plugin dependency and a render layer that native blocks avoid, and heavy locking frustrates power users. I'll take both costs: for structured components, field-based editing is safer than free-form block manipulation, and an ACF Pro dependency is a mature, acceptable risk for the control it buys. The fence can be widened later; un-breaking a brand is harder.
Field note: the number behind "accessible"Owning the markup centrally is also how accessibility stops being a cleanup project. At CyberArk I led an accessibility remediation across the entire WordPress site, taking our Lighthouse accessibility score from 46 to 95, and the lasting fix wasn't the audit, it was moving to components where semantics, contrast, and focus states are built in once and inherited everywhere. For a healthcare audience this isn't polish; WCAG conformance is a contractual reality with hospital and health-plan partners. In this build, every ACF Block ships accessible by default, and the score is held at 95 or better in the same CI budget as performance. The two disciplines reinforce each other: the semantic markup, proper image handling, and lean DOM that accessibility demands are the same fundamentals fast pages are made of.

5Section 5 · Lead gating

Lead gating without the performance tax

Gating strategy is a funnel decision; gating implementation is a performance decision. Both matter here.

Soft gates by default, hard gates by exception

ContentGateWhy
Case studies & papersSoft: full abstract and key findings visible; form unlocks the PDFThe visible content does the SEO work and earns the exchange. Hard-gating everything zeroes out organic growth on 5,000 URLs.
Live webinars, benchmark reportsHard: registration requiredHigh perceived value supports the ask; time-bound events justify friction.
Returning known leadsNo gate: recognized via the Marketo (Munchkin) cookie; progressive profiling on next conversionNever make the same person pay twice. Marketo's progressive profiling is built for exactly this: each subsequent form asks two new questions instead of repeating five old ones.

Gate level is a per-resource field with sensible defaults by type: a marketing lever, not a code change. And it's an A/B testing surface: my hypothesis is soft gates win on net enterprise leads; I'd rather measure than argue.

The implementation: no script bloat, no layout shift

I've spent years implementing Marketo's embed stack on the web side (forms2.min.js plus the Munchkin tracker) and out of the box it's a classic Core Web Vitals killer: render-blocking script weight and a form that pops into an unsized container, shoving the page down. Two rules fix it:

  • Load nothing until intent. forms2.min.js loads only when the gate scrolls near the viewport (IntersectionObserver) or on click, then MktoForms2.loadForm() renders into the slot, never in the critical path. Munchkin loads deferred, after LCP; it's analytics, not content. The stricter variant: skip the embed entirely, ship a native styled form, and submit to Marketo server-side, with full markup control, zero third-party render cost.
  • Reserve the space. The gate container has an explicit min-height before the form exists, and Marketo's default form CSS is stripped (above the fold styles conflict anyway) in favor of the design system's own tokens. When the form arrives, nothing on the page moves. CLS contribution: zero.

Downstream, submissions flow through the standard Marketo → Salesforce sync: program-status changes drive lead lifecycle, and campaign attribution lands where the sales team already lives. The web layer's job is to hand marketing ops clean, deduplicated, consented records, not to invent its own pipeline.

6Section 6 · Measurement

How I'd know it's working

  • Performance: CrUX field data (not just lab): p75 LCP < 1.5 s, CLS < 0.05, TTFB < 200 ms, tracked per template. Lighthouse budget in CI blocks regressions before deploy, and holds accessibility at 95+ per template alongside the performance numbers.
  • Growth: organic entrances to resource URLs, gate view → submission rate by gate type and medical specialty, net-new enterprise domains captured, and lead → opportunity rate in Salesforce via Marketo campaign sync. That's the metric the growth team actually answers for.
  • Operations: resources published per month without developer involvement, and time-to-publish. If the content manager needs me, Section 4 has failed.

First experiment on day one of having traffic: soft vs. hard gate on the top ten case studies, judged on qualified leads, not raw submissions.

How I'd ship it

In three phases, each one usable on its own. Phase one: the foundation. Post type, taxonomies, templates, caching, and the token-locked editor, launched with the first hundred or so resources so real content pressure-tests the model early. Phase two: conversion. Gating, Marketo integration, and the analytics plumbing, shipped only once the pages themselves are fast, because a gate on a slow page just measures abandonment. Phase three: scale and iterate. Bulk migration of the full library, then the A/B program. No phase waits on a redesign, and the content manager is publishing independently from the first month.

Where I stand, generally

Since you asked about personal technical direction: I build on boring, proven technology and align with the platform's own roadmap instead of fighting it. I treat performance and accessibility as budgets enforced in the pipeline, not as projects you do once. I'd rather run the test than win the meeting. And I measure my own success by how rarely the people I build for need me after launch.
